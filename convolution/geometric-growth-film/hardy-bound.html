The average over a full turn

0:00 The Hardy norm measures a full turn. At one ellipse point, add the four complex arrows first. The squared length of their resultant is the energy at that angle. Move around the ellipse and watch that energy change. The colour follows the height of the energy curve. Average those squared values over the full turn. The square root of that mean is the Hardy norm.

0:21 Give equal angles equal weight. Divide the circle into equal angle steps, and mark the corresponding cells on the ellipse. Their arc lengths can differ. Read the energy in each cell. Its sample height times the angle step gives the area of a rectangle on the angular plot. As the steps get finer, the rectangle sum approaches the integral. Dividing by two pi gives the mean squared size.

0:45 Watch a cross term rotate. Multiply the sum of the four modes by its complex conjugate. The grid shows each mode paired with each conjugate mode. A diagonal pair has frequency difference zero, so its contribution stays fixed. An off-diagonal pair has a nonzero integer difference. Its phase completes full turns as theta runs from zero to two pi, and its angular average is zero.

1:10 Only squared lengths survive. After averaging the full grid, only four diagonal entries remain. Each is the square of one arrow length. This is Parseval's identity. Add the squares of five halves, three eighths, three over thirty-two, and five over one hundred twenty-eight. The exact mean squared size is about six point four zero one. Taking the square root gives a Hardy norm of about two point five three.

1:35 Square the reciprocal radial factors. For the cubic pair, eight and one eighth become sixty-four and one over sixty-four. Multiply each by the square of its weight to get its energy. The doubled exponents belong to the radius. The original boundary waves still have three crests each.

1:52 Drop one factor of each weight. Each colour starts with its exact Fourier energy, the squared weight times the doubled radial power. Keep its radial factor fixed. Replace the squared weight by the weight itself. Every weight lies between zero and one, so each bar can only grow. For example, the positive cubic term keeps its radial energy factor sixty-four while five sixteenths squared becomes five sixteenths.

2:18 Pair the doubled powers. Read these bars on one energy scale. The exact squared norm is about six point four zero one. After dropping one weight from each term, the sum is about twenty point eight zero two. Pair the reciprocal radial factors with twice the logarithmic radius. Pairing keeps that intermediate total. Replacing all frequency magnitudes by the extreme gives the squared ceiling, about thirty-two point zero zero eight. Now take square roots. The Hardy norm is about two point five three, and its upper bound is about five point six five eight.

2:53 Check the constant mode. At degree zero there is just one phase and it stays fixed. The output is always one, so the energy profile is flat at height one. Every equal-angle cell has the same energy. Its mean, its square root and both bounds are all one. As the radius tends to one, the ellipse collapses to the real interval. This is the limiting picture of the angular construction.

3:16 Orthonormal scaling changes the size. For the real-line orthonormal mode, start with two n plus one. Divide by two and take the square root. Multiply the polynomial and both norm bounds by that factor. Squared energies take its square.

3:31 Compare the two cubic graphs. At each angle, evaluate both polynomials at the same point on the same ellipse. The graphs show the real parts of those two values. For degree three, the scaling factor is the square root of seven halves. The heights are multiplied by that factor, and their zero crossings stay at the same angles. The full complex value and both of its norm bounds scale by the same factor.

3:56 Return to the real line. Here x runs from minus one to one along the horizontal axis of an ordinary polynomial graph. In the previous plot, the horizontal coordinate was the angle around the ellipse. P zero is the constant one. Its graph stays at the same height across the interval. P one equals x. It rises along the diagonal through the origin. P two is the quadratic. Its graph dips to minus one half at the centre and reaches one at both ends. P three is the cubic. It crosses the horizontal axis three times and has two interior turning points. Compare P three with its orthonormal version. Multiply the height at each x by the square root of seven halves. The zeros and turning points keep their horizontal positions, while the extreme heights are scaled by that same factor.
