From a circle to an ellipse

0:00 What changes when the circle grows? On the real interval, the usual Legendre polynomials stayed within one in absolute value. Keep the polynomial and move the circle outward.

0:11 Begin with a point on a circle. Its radius is rho and its angle is theta. Fix rho at two. As theta changes, z travels around the circle.

0:21 The reciprocal runs the other way. Its radius is one half, and its angle is minus theta. Join the two moving points and mark their midpoint.

0:30 The midpoint traces an ellipse. The Joukowski map sends z to this midpoint, omega. Here on the ellipse is where we evaluate the polynomial.

0:38 Read the two semiaxes. At angle zero, the midpoint is five quarters to the right of the origin. After a quarter turn, the two points lie on opposite sides of the real axis. Their midpoint is three quarters above it.

0:51 The interval is the limiting picture. At radius one the vertical semiaxis vanishes, leaving the real interval. Increase rho beyond one and the ellipse opens up again.

Length and direction

1:12 Each power has a frequency. Term j has frequency n minus twice j. At degree three the frequencies are three, one, minus one and minus three.

1:21 Radius sets the arrow length. The length is the weight times rho to the power m. Positive powers grow as rho increases, negative powers shrink, and the zero frequency keeps its weight.

1:33 Angle sets the direction. An arrow turns through m times theta. Positive frequencies turn anticlockwise, negative ones clockwise. A zero frequency stays still.

The average over a full turn

6:46 The Hardy norm measures a full turn. At one ellipse point, add the four complex arrows first. The squared length of their resultant is the energy at that angle. Move around the ellipse and watch that energy change. The colour follows the height of the energy curve. Average those squared values over the full turn. The square root of that mean is the Hardy norm.

7:07 Give equal angles equal weight. Divide the circle into equal angle steps, and mark the corresponding cells on the ellipse. Their arc lengths can differ. Read the energy in each cell. Its sample height times the angle step gives the area of a rectangle on the angular plot. As the steps get finer, the rectangle sum approaches the integral. Dividing by two pi gives the mean squared size.

7:31 Watch a cross term rotate. Multiply the sum of the four modes by its complex conjugate. The grid shows each mode paired with each conjugate mode. A diagonal pair has frequency difference zero, so its contribution stays fixed. An off-diagonal pair has a nonzero integer difference. Its phase completes full turns as theta runs from zero to two pi, and its angular average is zero.

7:57 Only squared lengths survive. After averaging the full grid, only four diagonal entries remain. Each is the square of one arrow length. This is Parseval's identity.
