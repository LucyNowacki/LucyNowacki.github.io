Recover one real-line value

0:00 Can a loop recover one value? Take the function omega squared. Our target is its value at x equal to one half, namely one quarter. Cauchy's formula recovers this number from a counterclockwise circuit.

0:12 Place a pole at the target. Divide the function by omega minus x. The denominator vanishes at one half, while the numerator there is one quarter. This gives a genuine simple pole.

0:23 Start with a small circle. Its centre is x. Take its radius epsilon to be one quarter for the drawing. Any sufficiently small positive radius works if the circle and its interior lie inside the ellipse.

0:37 The tangent cancels the denominator. On the circle, omega minus x is epsilon times e to the i t. The differential contributes i times that same factor times d t. After dividing by two pi i, only the function value times d t over two pi remains.

0:55 Keep the constant part. Expand the square along the circle. One term is constant, one turns once, and another turns twice. Both rotating terms average to zero over a full turn. What survives is x squared, or one quarter.

Apply the Legendre weight

4:56 Now recover a coefficient. A Legendre coefficient combines values from the whole real interval. For degree two, multiply x squared by P two of x and integrate from minus one to one.

5:08 Include the normalization. The classical Legendre polynomial equals one at the right endpoint. Its coefficient formula carries a factor of n plus one half. For degree two, that factor is five halves.

5:21 Keep the sign of the weight. P two is one half of the quantity three x squared minus one. Its zeros are plus and minus one over the square root of three. The product is negative between them except at zero, and positive toward both ends.

5:36 Add the signed areas. Integrating x squared times P two gives four fifteenths. Multiply by five halves and the coefficient is two thirds. This is the number we will now recover from the ellipse.

Collect the Cauchy kernels

5:49 Give every interval point its own kernel. Replace each f of x in the real integral by its Cauchy formula. As x moves along the interval, the factor one over omega minus x follows it.

6:01 Exchange the two integrations. The ellipse boundary stays a positive distance from the entire interval. The parametrised integrand is continuous on a compact rectangle, so Fubini's theorem lets us integrate over x first.

6:16 The kernels combine into one function. The bracket now integrates P two of x divided by omega minus x. By definition, half of that bracket is Q two of omega, so the whole bracket is twice Q two.

6:29 Follow the coefficient around the ellipse. The factor of two from the bracket turns five halves into five. Multiply omega squared by Q two, by the oriented differential, and by five over two pi i. The accumulated value can be complex partway round. At the end it reaches two thirds.

6:48 Keep the kernel off the slit. Q two is holomorphic off the real segment from minus one to one. The coefficient contour surrounds that entire segment. Deforming it across the slit would leave the region where this kernel is holomorphic.
