Connection to the thesis

This lesson follows Subsection 1.2.1 — Joukowski–Legendre Laurent expansion, in Section 1.2, Legendre coefficient bounds and projection tails on Bernstein ellipses.

Lemma 1.17 — Joukowski–Legendre Laurent expansion. For \(z\in\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}\) and \(n\in\mathbb N_0\),

\[P_n(\mathit{Jouk}(z))=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\alpha_{n,j}z^{n-2j},\qquad \alpha_{n,j}=\alpha_j\alpha_{n-j}.\]

The thesis attributes this identity to Bandtlow’s EDMD–Legendre Lemma 1.1. That is the cited source’s numbering; Lemma 1.17 is its number in the thesis. The manuscript remains the mathematical source.

The walkthrough uses the thesis notation: generating variable \(s\), circle radius \(\varrho\), and frequency \(m=n-2j\). The existing exploration views use the equivalent names \(u=s\), \(r=\varrho\), and \(k=m\). We study the unscaled mode \(P_n\) here.

The underlying Joukowski map

\(z=re^{it}\ \longmapsto\ \Gamma_r(t)=\frac12(z+z^{-1})\)

The circle in the z-plane maps to the ellipse in the ω-plane. These are coordinates where the polynomial is evaluated; the plots above show the values of its components.

Why this sum is a convolution.

The auxiliary variable u keeps track of degree. Multiplying the two series combines degrees p and q; extracting uⁿ selects exactly p + q = n.

01

Factor the quadratic

\(1-2\omega u+u^2=(1-zu)(1-z^{-1}u)\)

Substitute 2ω = z + z⁻¹, then group:
(1 − zu) − z⁻¹u(1 − zu).

02

Expand each factor

\((1-zu)^{-1/2}=\sum_{p\ge0}\alpha_p z^p u^p\)
\((1-z^{-1}u)^{-1/2}=\sum_{q\ge0}\alpha_q z^{-q}u^q\)
03

Match the degree

\([u^n]\sum_{p,q\ge0}\alpha_p\alpha_q z^{p-q}u^{p+q}\)

Set q = j, p = n − j. The sum p + q fixes n; the difference p − q becomes n − 2j.

The tail bound concerns truncation, not floating-point rounding. Both analytic branches equal 1 at u = 0. Here u is real, 0 ≤ u < 1/r, so both binomial expansions converge absolutely.

Where do the coefficients αⱼ come from?

Taylor-expand (1 − v)⁻¹ᐟ² at zero. Repeated differentiation gives

\(\frac{d^j}{dv^j}(1-v)^{-1/2}=\frac{1\cdot3\cdots(2j-1)}{2^j}(1-v)^{-j-1/2}.\)

Dividing the derivative at zero by j! gives the Taylor coefficient (the empty product is 1):

\(\alpha_j=\frac{1\cdot3\cdots(2j-1)}{2^j j!}=\frac{(2j)!}{4^j(j!)^2}=\frac{\binom{2j}{j}}{4^j}.\)
\(\alpha_0=1,\quad\alpha_{j+1}=\frac{2j+1}{2j+2}\alpha_j,\quad(1-v)^{-1/2}=1+\frac12v+\frac38v^2+\frac5{16}v^3+\cdots\)

The ratio of successive terms tends to |v|, so the series converges absolutely for |v| < 1. Analyticity of the branch at zero identifies its Taylor sum with the function.

Keep the amplitude separate from the shape.

\(\underbrace{a_n(f)}_{\text{one mode's amplitude}}\,\underbrace{P_n(\Gamma_r(t))}_{\text{mode shape}}=\sum_{j=0}^n\underbrace{a_n(f)\alpha_j\alpha_{n-j}r^{n-2j}}_{\text{complex Fourier coefficient}}\,\underbrace{e^{i(n-2j)t}}_{\text{rotating phase}}\)

The pull-back changes the representation of Pₙ, while its Legendre coefficient stays the same. Several Legendre modes can share a Fourier frequency; their contributions add in the full function.

How aₙ(f) is obtained
\(a_n(f)=\frac{2n+1}{2}\int_{-1}^1 f(x)P_n(x)\,dx\)

For f ∈ L²(−1,1), these are the Legendre coefficients on the interval. Evaluating the full expansion on an ellipse additionally requires an analytic extension and convergence there. This page explores a single polynomial mode, whose finite identity is valid for every z ≠ 0.
