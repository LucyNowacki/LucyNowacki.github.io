Why is this the first possible mode count?

Keeping one more mode removes the positive term \((2N+1)q^N\) from \(\mathsf T_N(q)\). The first count with \(\mathsf T_N(q)\leq\varepsilon\) therefore meets the target, while the preceding count exceeds it. For the displayed polynomial family the corresponding function bound is \(A\,\mathsf T_N(q)\).

What to try. Hold the radii fixed and increase \(N\). Then move the inner ellipse closer to the outer one. Watch how many terms are needed to make the scalar tail small.

Earlier ingredients. Geometric growth bounds each Legendre mode. Bounded analytic coefficients and Hardy coefficients supply the decay that outweighs that growth.

Intuitive/geometric explanation

The larger ellipse supplies a coefficient factor \(R^{-n}\), and reconstruction on the smaller ellipse costs at most \(r^n\). Their product is \(q^n\). The coefficient estimate also contributes \(2n+1\), producing the scalar majorant \(b_n=(2n+1)q^n\).

The weighted coefficient norm is \(\|c\|_{\ell^1_r}=\sum_{n\ge0}|c_n|r^n\). The factor \(2n+1\) belongs to the coefficient estimate, rather than to the definition of this norm. Summing the weighted magnitudes bounds the reconstructed function on the whole target ellipse.

The finite example uses \(c_n=A(2n+1)2^{-n}e^{-in\alpha}\) for \(0\le n\le47\). Changing the phase control changes this polynomial. Here \(A\) controls the coefficient envelope, while the outer Hardy reading is a diagnostic measurement.

The moving hump records a local squared modulus, while the lighthouse records a local modulus. A full Hardy norm requires the normalized angular average and a square root. The schematic hump shape does not represent the entire boundary profile.

Why the estimate follows

The bound \(\|P_n\|_{A(E_r)}\le r^n\) makes the Weierstrass majorant test applicable whenever \(c\in\ell^1_r\). Thus synthesis \(\mathcal S_rc=\sum c_nP_n\) has norm at most one into \(A(E_r)\), \(H^\infty(E_r)\), and \(H^2(E_r)\).

\[\begin{aligned}\mathsf S_N(q)&=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(2n+1)q^n,\\\mathsf T_N(q)&=\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}(2n+1)q^n.\end{aligned}\]
\[\mathsf T_N(q)=q^N\!\left(\frac{2N+1}{1-q}+\frac{2q}{(1-q)^2}\right).\]

Applying \(2q\,d/dq+1\) to the geometric series gives these exact scalar sums. In particular, \(\mathsf S_N+\mathsf T_N=\mathsf S_\infty=(1+q)/(1-q)^2\). A finite displayed set of bars does not remove the remaining infinite tail.

These scalar equalities produce upper bounds for function and operator norms. They do not assert that every coefficient estimate is attained simultaneously. The illustrative polynomial and the infinite scalar majorant therefore have separate readings.

Connection to the thesis

This lesson follows “Embedding bookkeeping, synthesis, and exact tail sums” in the Distilled section “Two Hardy embeddings and Legendre projection tails.” It packages the preceding coefficient and mode estimates for the next two restrictions.

The real-line coefficients are \(a_n(f)=(n+\tfrac12)\int_{-1}^{1}f(x)P_n(x)\,dx\). Hence \(\mathcal P_Nf=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}a_n(f)P_n\), with \(\mathcal P_0=0\). This projector differs from the later Hardy orthogonal projector \(\mathcal P_N^X\), even though both retain the same polynomial space.

The full sum supplies embedding constants, and the scalar tail supplies a projection-error comparator. This scalar \(\mathsf T_N(q)\) is distinct from the finite connection matrix denoted \(\mathsf T_N(r)\) later in the thesis.
